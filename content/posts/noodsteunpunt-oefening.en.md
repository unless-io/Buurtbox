A noodsteunpunt is a local emergency support point where residents can go for help during a large-scale crisis. When I read that the municipality of Alkmaar was running a full exercise at community centre Mare Nostrum, I reached out to ask if I could join.

<!--more-->

I had come across the exercise through a local news article. The municipality was testing how their noodsteunpunt would function during a prolonged power outage. I contacted the coordinator by email and asked if I could participate. She was happy to have me and was intrigued by the Buurtbox project. I was registered as a *figurant*: a volunteer playing the role of a resident with a problem to solve.

For me this was a research exercise, not a Buurtbox field test. I was there to observe, ask questions, and understand how a noodsteunpunt actually operates under pressure.

![Flag marking the noodsteunpunt at Mare Nostrum.](/images/posts/noodsteunpunt-oefening/flag.jpg)

## Arriving at the noodsteunpunt

The scenario was a prolonged power outage across the region. When I arrived at Mare Nostrum I could already see the building was running on a generator.

![Generator running outside the noodsteunpunt.](/images/posts/noodsteunpunt-oefening/generator.jpg)

At the front door I was stopped — the setup was not quite ready. We were directed around the building to a large gym. I sat down with the other figurants and we chatted while waiting for the briefing.

In the email briefing we had received beforehand we were told to come up with our own scenario. During the in-person briefing it became clear we would actually receive role cards, each with a specific situation and instructions on how to play it and when to escalate.

![Signboard at the entrance marking the noodsteunpunt.](/images/posts/noodsteunpunt-oefening/signboard.jpg)

Figurants were released in groups of four. I was in the first group and we walked up to the main entrance together.

## The layout of the noodsteunpunt

At the entrance the sliding doors were open. In the hallway stood a group of *handhaving* officers — municipal enforcement officers acting as security. They seemed a little unsure of what to do. I looked their way expecting to be directed, but nothing happened, so I walked on into the building.

At the end of the first corridor was a main desk. A staff member heard my situation and directed me further. As I discovered over the course of the afternoon, this main desk functioned as a triage point: quickly assessing each visitor and routing them to the right team.

The noodsteunpunt had several key areas:

- **Main desk** — intake and triage for all visitors
- **Information desk** — general questions and practical information
- **112 area** — situations requiring immediate assistance from police, fire, or medical teams
- **Salvation Army area** — coordinating public initiatives and collective resources
- **Handhaving area** — enforcement-related questions
- **Security team** — managing the entrance and exit

![Sticker on the entrance door marking the noodsteunpunt.](/images/posts/noodsteunpunt-oefening/sticker.jpg)

## First scenario: medications for 80 elderly residents

My first role card gave me this situation: I had started a neighbourhood initiative to distribute medications to 80 elderly residents. I had a list of their names and what they needed. I had gone to the pharmacy to pick up the medications, but was refused. The pharmacy itself was unable to process deliveries during the outage. I arrived at the noodsteunpunt requesting an official letter of endorsement that would compel the pharmacy to release the medications to me.

My mission was to convince the staff to issue that letter.

From the main desk I was directed to the information desk. The desk worker heard me out but told me they could not grant permission to collect medications on behalf of all 80 residents. After going back and forth on this for a while, we landed on a reasonable outcome: I could return to my neighbourhood, collect signatures from the most urgent cases, and use those to pick up medication for them specifically. Anyone in immediate medical need could be helped by the 112 team. Everyone else would have to wait, though we could try to gather signatures for them too.

It was a sensible resolution given the circumstances. I exited through the back of the building and returned to the gym to receive my next role card.

## Other scenarios

I worked through several more roles over the course of the afternoon:

- **A camping site manager** with a large group of German guests, all asking whether it was safe to travel home and what conditions were like in Germany.
- **A former primary school teacher** offering to teach a small group of five children at home.
- **A nearby neighbourhood centre manager** offering to open the doors of his venue as a shelter for up to 50 people.
- **A person who had spotted a fire** — a burning paper container next to a parked tour bus nearby.

I was also given the chance to play a scenario of my own choosing: my wife was heavily pregnant, labour had just started, I could not reach the midwife, and I needed help immediately. This was the most chaotic scenario I went through, but I was ultimately helped adequately.

![Main entrance to Mare Nostrum.](/images/posts/noodsteunpunt-oefening/entrance.jpg)

## Two sessions, one key change

The afternoon was split into two sessions with a break in between. A few things changed between them.

In the second session there was a noticeably stronger security presence at the door, and many people were turned away before they could enter. Another change involved the information board.

During the first session, an information board in the first hallway went almost completely unnoticed. In the second session it was moved to a more prominent position behind the main desk.

![The information board at the noodsteunpunt.](/images/posts/noodsteunpunt-oefening/information-board.jpg)

The board contained regularly updated information: supermarkets were closed, public transport had stopped, the two main tunnel routes south — Wijker and Velser — were closed. It listed the time of the next update and the radio frequency for emergency broadcasts.

That radio frequency actually came up during one of the scenarios, and most noodsteunpunt staff did not know it. It took asking several people before it was found — written on that same board. So the staff were also missing out on the information available to them.

## What I observed

The gym, where figurants waited between roles, became an informal gauge of how well things were going. By the second half of the day the mood in the gym was notably frustrated. Many people had been turned away at the entrance and several figurants were genuinely worried about what a real outage would mean for residents.

The whole operation was fundamentally an exercise in funnel management and resource allocation. The 112 desk was the busiest, most needed, and most complex area. The central challenge was keeping it from being overwhelmed: making sure only people in genuine need reached it, while everyone else was helped elsewhere.

Turning people away at the door worked against that goal. It quickly soured the mood and you could sense the beginnings of a crowd turning hostile among those who felt dismissed. What people need is to feel that someone will listen to their story and take it seriously.

The tensest moment came when the hallway filled with people waiting to be helped. One of the professional actors — who I later found out was one of the organizers — was deliberately escalating the situation. She was in the queue, pacing back and forth, complaining loudly, and wandering into areas of the building where visitors were not meant to go.

![A handhaving van parked outside the noodsteunpunt.](/images/posts/noodsteunpunt-oefening/handhaving-van.jpg)

## Conclusion

My main takeaway is that civilian resilience is essential to making a noodsteunpunt function well under pressure.

First: make sure people only come to the noodsteunpunt if they genuinely cannot solve their problem otherwise. Second: when people do arrive, make information clear and accessible enough for them to resolve things themselves. Third: when staff do need to intervene, make triage fast and resource management effective.

The exercise also showed something else: the real bottleneck in a crisis is not only the emergency support point itself, but the information flow around it. People arrive confused, under stress, and often without the right information to decide whether they should turn up at all. Staff also need clear, reliable updates on road closures, services, shelter options, and emergency communications. This is where Buurtbox fits.

Buurtbox is not meant to replace the noodsteunpunt or formal emergency services. Rather, it can strengthen the local crisis response layer around them. It can act as a trusted neighbourhood information hub: current updates, practical guidance, local contacts, and easy access to the information people need when normal communication channels fail. It can also support staff by making the most important information visible and easy to share, especially in chaotic and fast-moving situations.

For me, the most important lesson from this exercise was that emergency resilience depends on both formal institutions and local self-organisation. The noodsteunpunt is the front line. Buurtbox is the local infrastructure that can help communities stay informed, coordinated, and resilient before, during, and after disruption.
